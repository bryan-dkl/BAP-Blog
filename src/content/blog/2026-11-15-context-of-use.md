## Context of use ##

### Why we mapped the context? ###
The personas tell us who plays. But the same game can work well in a quiet studio and fail at a crowded festival for example. So we described the situation our installation will actually be used in, split into 5 parts.

### 1.Users ###
Our primary users are children aged 9-12, playing in groups of 2 to 4. Around them are parents, siblings or teachers who watch or join in. (There's also a "3rd group" we can't forget: the JEF volunteers who supervise and have to step in when something goes wrong.)

### 2.Goal ###
The kids want to have fun together with their friends or family. They come for the festival as a whole and not for our installation in particular, so we have to earn their attention. Parents and teachers mostly want the kids to enjoy themselves safely.

### 3.Physical environment ###
The installation stands in the XL-Medialab at JEF, sharing a room with several other installations. It will be busy and noisy, with sound and screens coming from all sides. Visitors walk in and out freely, and nobody explains things to them.

### 4.Social environment ###
Kids come with family, friends or school class. Other groups and strangers stand around watching or waiting. That means kids are always aware of being seen, by peers and by adults. The volunteers running the room have little time and no technical background.

### 5.Time ###
The festival runs from 28 January to 14 February 2027, with around 5,000 visitors. School classes come on weekdays, families on Wednesday afternoons, weekends and holidays. Each group only spends a short time per installation, especially school classes. And the installation has to run all day for about three weeks without a developer around.

### What this means for the design ###
A few things became very clear from this:

- The game has to explain itself, because nobody else will.
- It has to stand out in a loud room, with visuals that work from a distance and sound that cuts through.
- Rounds have to be short enough for school classes on a schedule.
- It has to be robust and easy to restart, so a volunteer can fix things without any technical knowledge.
- Because there's always an audience, playing in front of others has to feel good and not embarrassing.
